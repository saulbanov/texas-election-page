Where the races stand

The Senate race turns on one unknown: how the 2.3 million early voters who have never voted in a party primary, the no-primary voters, are voting. Each card runs the same early vote through a different answer, most of them taken from a past election.

Our central read for the U.S. Senate

The middle card is our central read, and it is the conservative one. The cards on either side are the tails, with the same early vote and a different break among voters with no primary history.     The group is above the last midterm and below the last presidential year. Over a whole election, 2018 had 3.85M of these voters and 2022 had 3.06M. We expect about 4M, a little past 2018.

    Measured Ryan's 2018 report covers only the 15 largest counties, which allows one direct comparison at day 8. In those counties, Republican primary voters led in 2018, . In the same counties in 2026, Democratic primary voters lead, . Part of that swing is relabeling: Democratic primaries were small before 2018, and the record primaries since then moved many Democrats out of the no-primary group. The counts can't separate the two.

    Two groups that get confused

    Measured Through Thursday, early voters have no record of voting anywhere. Another have voted in general elections but never in a primary. Together they are 33.7% of the early vote, and 49% of Thursday's voters.

    What the sources say about them

    • Measured Ryan's definition: people who just turned 18, people new to Texas, and registered voters who have sat out recent elections.
    • Measured The early electorate as a whole is younger (ages 18–29 up 4.2 points on 2022), more Hispanic (up 3.0 points, to 18.4%) and more female (women lead by ).
    • A gap. No source on file breaks out the age, race or location of the no-primary voters themselves. The figures above describe all early voters.

    What we assume

    Assumed They lean D +5 to +12, centered on D +8.5. Three things point that way. Hispanic polling this year runs D +10 to +18, the early electorate is younger and more female, and state legislative specials since January 2025 have averaged D +5.6. Two point the other way. In 2022 these voters leaned about R +4. In 2024 they split their tickets: about R +4 for president and for Senate. Ryan describes the group as "center and/or left" in general, which is a description and not a 2026 measurement.

    Why the polls might miss them

    Likely-voter screens filter out people without a voting record. In 2018 the final polls had Cruz ahead by 6 to 9 points; he won by 2.6. In 2020 and 2024, Texas polls missed the other way, underestimating Republicans.

    How it could be wilder than any recent year

    • Size. At presidential scale, 5M of these voters, every point of lean is worth 50,000 votes instead of 40,000.
    • Lean. It could run past our range either way. 2018 is not a ceiling: Hispanic polling this year runs as high as D +18, past anything in our central read. Trump's occasional voters pull the other way.
    • Splitting. New voters skip down-ballot races more often, and Texas has no straight-ticket option. The Senate and the races below it can diverge.
    The weakest number on this page The lean. It is assumed, nothing in the turnout data measures it, and it moves the Senate race about 3 points across its range. Everything in the turnout counts so far has moved it less than a point.
    How we estimated 2024 From the 2024 early vote by county on the Secretary of State’s site, set against Ryan’s day-8 mix of 2024 early voters. Using a day-8 mix for the whole early vote makes it rough. The same method gives R +3.1 for 2022, close to the R +4 we use.

    What could happen

    No recent Texas election looks like this one. At this point in 2022 and 2024, Republican primary voters led the early vote by hundreds of thousands. This year Democratic primary voters lead. Past years give a range, not an answer. Most of these races will turn on the 2.3 million early voters who have never voted in a primary, and the turnout data can't show how they are voting.

    These are five readings, not a forecast. Our central read is the conservative one. Nothing in the early vote caps Democrats at their 2018 level, and a blowout like the last card would not show in the turnout numbers before the count. Try your own assumptions in the workbench.

    About this page

    Who publishes this

    Future Heist is a Texas-based organization that tells stories with data, particularly about the oil and gas industry, which is on the ballot this year more than any other industry. We built this page to show a historic election in action, and to show what the early vote can and can't tell us. Counts come from Texas Secretary of State voter records, compiled by Transparency USA and Ryan Data & Research. Our read comes from our synthesis of past elections and of other experts' reads. Gold marks our assumptions. This is a rough model, not a forecast. We are in uncharted territory, folks.

    No one who worked on this page has worked for, advised, endorsed or been paid by any candidate or party in these races.

    To keep ourselves honest, and to avoid claiming we saw something coming that we didn't, we committed our predictions and assumptions to a repository before the data arrived, with timestamps, and that repository is now public. We keep a corrections log, our model and code are public, and the page uses aggregate counts only. Send corrections to saul@futureheist.org.
