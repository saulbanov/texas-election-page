        Step 2 of 7

        Primary voters vote with their party

        Assumed We use 95% loyalty

        About 95% of voters who have cast a party's primary ballot back that party's candidates in November. That is Ryan Data & Research's rule of thumb, and it is why the primary-voter count matters. Each 100,000 votes of primary-voter lead is worth about 0.9 points.

        So far the two parties' primary voters are close to even, of each other. That is new. At the same point in 2022 Republican primary voters led by 436,528, and in 2024 by 794,322.

        Step 3 of 7

        Reliable Republicans still to vote

        Assumed We use R +200,000 on Election Day

        More reliable Republicans than Democrats are still waiting to vote. Counting people who voted in three or four of the last four primaries and have not voted yet, Republicans lead by . We assume Election Day adds a net 200,000 Republican primary voters, about that gap plus the usual Republican tilt of Election Day.

        This is the number Tuesday afternoon tests. Heavy turnout in Republican-base counties would mean more; light turnout, less. The scenario dial above runs 100,000, 200,000 and 350,000.

        Step 4 of 7

        How the no-primary voters break

        Assumed We use D +3.3

        This is the assumption the projection leans on most. We start from the one Texas measurement we have. Among early voters in the 2024 Senate race, these voters split almost evenly, favoring the Democrat by about , while favoring Trump by about 8. We add part of the shift toward Democrats since then, about 3 points. State legislative specials since January 2025 have run about 5.6 points better for Democrats, and Univision measures Texas Hispanic voters about 11 points more Democratic than in 2024, but neither measures these Texas voters directly. That lands near D +3.3.

        The case against it is that in 2022 these voters leaned about R +4, and the post-2024 shift is measured mostly outside Texas. The case for it is that this is a midterm under a Republican president, when the president's occasional voters tend to stay home, and the group is smaller and likelier to be committed voters than in 2024. Ryan describes these voters in general as "center and/or left."

        The weakest number on this page The lean. It is assumed, nothing in the turnout data measures it, and it moves the Senate race about 3 points across its range. Everything in the turnout counts so far has moved it less than a point.
        How we estimated 2024 From the 2024 early vote by county on the Secretary of State’s site, set against Ryan’s day-12 mix of 2024 early voters, close to the whole early vote. Until Nov. 1 we used his day-8 mix, which gave D +5.4 and put our central lean at D +8.5. The same method gives D +2.3 among 2022’s early voters, against about R +3 for all 2022 voters, so early voters with no primary history lean more Democratic than the group as a whole.
        Step 5 of 7

        How many of them vote

        Assumed We use about 4 million

        Over a whole election, 2018 had 3.85 million voters with no primary history and 2022 had 3.06 million. With already in the early vote, we expect about 4 million, a little past 2018. Every point of their lean is worth about 40,000 votes at that size, and 50,000 at a presidential-year 5 million.

        Step 6 of 7

        From the Senate to the other races

        Assumed We use candidate adjustments and drop-off

        Each statewide race starts from the Senate figure. Paxton runs about 4 points behind a typical Republican, so a typical Democrat-versus-Republican race sits 4 points less Democratic than the Senate. In the final New York Times/Siena poll of Texas, Talarico led Paxton by 7 and Hinojosa led Abbott by 1, a 6-point gap; Abbott’s own edge accounts for about 2 of it. About 503,000 Republicans voted for John Cornyn against Paxton in the May runoff. Each race then moves by its candidates’ polling: Abbott runs about 2 points ahead, Goodwin about 1 ahead after halving, Middleton about 1 ahead. Agriculture and the Railroad Commission have no general-election polling.

        New voters skip races lower on the ballot more often, and Texas has no straight-ticket option, so the Lt. Governor, Attorney General, Agriculture and Railroad Commission races each lose 0.75 to 1 point for Democrats. The workbench also lets you set ticket-splitting.

        For the Texas House and Senate, each district starts from its 2024 result: its own House race where a Democrat ran against a Republican, and the 2024 U.S. Senate race everywhere else. Every district then moves by the same amount as the statewide races, with the same 1-point drop-off as Agriculture. Some Republican incumbents ran well ahead of Cruz in 2024, and starting from their own races keeps that edge where they are running again. The 26 open seats, where no incumbent is on the ballot, lose it and start from the Senate race. With no shift from 2024, this gives Democrats 63 House seats; they hold 62. Senate districts start from the 2024 Senate race, which puts Taylor Rehmet’s SD-9 on the Republican side in our central read, though he won it in a special election in January. Seats with no Republican on the ballot count for Democrats. District figures come from the Texas Legislative Council.

        Step 7 of 7

        How sure we are

        Assumed We use a miss of about 2.3 points either way

        The odds on this page assume the model can miss by about 2.3 points in either direction. A miss that size covers a lean anywhere from about D +3 to D +14. A 2022-style break is outside it.

        Why the polls might miss them

        Likely-voter screens filter out people without a voting record. In 2018 the final polls had Cruz ahead by 6 to 9 points; he won by 2.6. In 2020 and 2024, Texas polls missed the other way, underestimating Republicans.

        About this page

        Who publishes this

        Future Heist is a Texas-based organization that tells stories with data, particularly about the oil and gas industry, which is on the ballot this year more than any other industry. We built this page to show a historic election in action, and to show what the early vote can and can't tell us. Counts come from Texas Secretary of State voter records, compiled by Transparency USA and Ryan Data & Research. Our read comes from our synthesis of past elections and of other experts' reads. Gold marks our assumptions. This is a rough model, not a forecast. We are in uncharted territory, folks.

        No one who worked on this page has worked for, advised, endorsed or been paid by any candidate or party in these races.

        To keep ourselves honest, and to avoid claiming we saw something coming that we didn't, we committed our predictions and assumptions to a repository before the data arrived, with timestamps, and that repository is now public. We keep a corrections log, our model and code are public, and the page uses aggregate counts only. Send corrections to saul@futureheist.org.
